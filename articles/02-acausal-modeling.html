Acausal Modeling of Hydropower Systems

This note develops the mathematical meaning of acausal, equation-based modeling for a hydropower plant. The central idea is that physical components are written from conservation laws and constitutive relations, without assigning a fixed input-output direction before the system is assembled.

1. Causal and acausal forms

A causal block is written explicitly as

$$y=\phi(u).$$

The computational direction is fixed: $u$ is known and $y$ is computed. In an acausal component we instead write

$$F(y,u,\theta)=0.$$

Neither variable is declared to be the input. Once many components are connected, the solver determines a consistent set of unknowns.

2. Hydraulic ports

A hydraulic connector may carry a potential variable, head or pressure, and a flow variable. For two connected components, continuity requires

$$Q_1+Q_2=0,$$

while equality of potential gives

$$H_1=H_2.$$

A pipe may have a constitutive relation

$$L\dot Q=gA\left(H_a-H_b-KQ|Q|\right).$$

The same pipe can experience either positive or negative flow; no permanent signal direction is required.

3. Mechanical rotational ports

For an ideal shaft connection, angular velocity is shared,

$$\omega_1=\omega_2,$$

and torques satisfy action-reaction balance,

$$\tau_1+\tau_2=0.$$

A rotating inertia obeys

$$J\dot\omega=\tau_m-\tau_e-D(\omega-\omega_s).$$

Again, torque need not be pre-labelled as an input or output at the connection level.

4. Electrical ports

At an electrical node, voltage is common,

$$v_1=v_2=\cdots=v_n,$$

and Kirchhoff current balance gives

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} i_k=0.$$

Generator, load, and network models are therefore naturally assembled as simultaneous algebraic constraints.

5. Component equations and connection equations

Suppose component $i$ is described by

$$F_i(\dot x_i,x_i,z_i,p_i)=0,$$

where $p_i$ denotes port variables. The complete plant is formed by stacking component equations and connection equations:

$$\mathcal F(\dot x,x,z,p)=0,$$

together with

$$\mathcal C(p)=0.$$

After elimination of redundant port variables, the plant can be represented as

$$F(\dot x,x,z,u,\theta)=0.$$

6. Why DAEs appear

Many physical variables are constrained instantaneously. They do not possess independent differential equations. If

$$\dot x=f(x,z,u),$$

and

$$0=g(x,z,u),$$

then $x$ are differential states while $z$ are algebraic variables. This is a differential-algebraic equation system.

7. Structural causality is assigned later

Although a physical model is written acausally, numerical solution eventually requires a computational ordering. Around a regular operating point, algebraic equations can locally be solved when

$$\det\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial z}\right)\neq0.$$

Then, by the implicit-function theorem, there exists locally

$$z=\psi(x,u).$$

Substituting into the differential equations gives

$$\dot x=f(x,\psi(x,u),u).$$

Thus computational causality is a property of the solved equation system, not necessarily of the way each physical component was authored.

8. Acausal does not mean uncontrolled

A controller can be connected to an acausal plant. For example, a governor may generate guide-vane position $y_g$ from measured speed,

$$y_g=C(\omega_{\rm ref}-\omega),$$

while the turbine still satisfies an acausal constitutive equation such as

$$0=Q-C_dA_g(y_g)\sqrt{2gH_t}.$$

The plant remains equation based; the controller contributes additional equations.

9. Energetic interpretation

Physical ports often consist of one effort variable and one flow variable. Their product is power. Examples are

$$P_h=\rho gHQ,$$

for hydraulics,

$$P_m=\tau\omega,$$

for mechanics, and

$$P_e=vi,$$

for electrical systems. This common structure explains why acausal multi-domain modeling is especially natural for hydropower plants.

10. Residual formulation

A convenient universal representation is

$$\boxed{R(t,\dot y,y,u,\theta)=0}.$$

This single residual form can contain ODEs, algebraic equations, constitutive laws, and connection constraints.

11. Operating-point linearization

At equilibrium,

$$R(0,0,y^\star,u^\star,\theta)=0.$$

For perturbations $\Delta y$ and $\Delta u$, first-order expansion gives

$$E\,\Delta\dot y=A\,\Delta y+B\,\Delta u,$$

where

$$E=\left.\frac{\partial R}{\partial \dot y}\right|_\star,\qquad A=-\left.\frac{\partial R}{\partial y}\right|_\star,\qquad B=-\left.\frac{\partial R}{\partial u}\right|_\star.$$

If $E$ is singular, the linearized system remains a descriptor system. This is the natural bridge from acausal physical modeling to small-signal control analysis.

12. Main conclusion

Acausal modeling concerns how physical equations are expressed and assembled. It is not the same concept as synchronous or asynchronous software execution. The physically meaningful distinction is between conservation-based component equations and an imposed input-output signal direction.
